\chapter{Simulation}

\section{Method}

The stationarity of sequences $\{S_j\}_{j\geq 1}$ and $\{A_j\}_{j\geq 1}$ forms the theoretical foundation for empirical simulation and analysis according to the following algorithm:
\needspace{18\baselineskip}
\underline{\textbf{Algorithm: Simulating the Sandpile Model}}\\
initialise $\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{\ell}, \mathbf{d}$ as empty arrays of size $n$ \\
initialise $\eta = \mathbf{0}$\\
\textbf{for} $i \in \{1, \cdots, m \}$ \textbf{do}: \\
simulate $\mathbf{X}_i$\\
$\eta \gets \delta(\eta, \mathbf{X}_i)$\\
$\eta \gets \xi(\eta)$\\
\textbf{end for}\\
\textbf{for} $i \in \{m + 1, \cdots, m + n \}$ \textbf{do}: \\
simulate $\mathbf{X}_i$\\
$\eta \gets \delta(\eta, \mathbf{X}_i)$\\
$\mathbf{s}[i-m] \gets s(\eta)$\\
$\mathbf{a}[i-m] \gets a(\eta)$\\
$\mathbf{\ell}[i-m] \gets \ell(\eta)$\\
$\mathbf{d}[i-m] \gets d(\eta)$\\
$\eta \gets \xi(\eta)$\\
\textbf{end for}\\
\textbf{return} $\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}$\\

In the above, simulated states $\eta_1, \cdots, \eta_m$ are called burn-in states, and are discarded. After this period, we assume that the distribution of $\eta_j$ is approximately stationary. Therefore, simulations $\eta_{m+1}, \cdots, \eta_{m+n}$ are retained and their empirical distribution approximates the stationary distribution $\pi(\eta)$. From that sequence, we construct simulated sequences $S_j$ and $A_j$ of length $n$, whose distribution approximates $p(S)$ and $p(A)$ respectively.

\section{Practicalities}
In practice, hundreds of thousands of simulations are required to approach stationarity. This is computationally expensive, especially for large lattice sizes, because computations cannot be parallelised due to the Markov structure of the problem. 
An initial Python implementation of the simulation procedure above was too slow to produce meaningful results. The final implementation uses \texttt{numba} with \texttt{njit} decorators, which stands for 'no python just in time' \cite{lam2015numba}. This compiles the Python code prior to execution, bypassing the CPython interpreter and achieving execution speed near code written in C. However, only a subset of Python and \texttt{numpy} functionality is permitted, requiring significant refactoring away from object-oriented design.

\section{Results}
Simulations were performed for lattice sizes $L \in \{5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 65, 80, 100\}$. The burn-in period was set to $m=4\times 10^4$ and sequences of length $n=1 \times 10^6$ were retained for each lattice size.

The plots in Figure \ref{fig:simulation_statistics} depect a linear trend in early values of avalanche size and avalanche area, suggesting a power law distribution followed by a sharp decay. The distributions for Loss and L1 Length do not appear to have this power law structure, and the remainder of this report focusses on understanding and analysing the distribution of avalanche size and area.
