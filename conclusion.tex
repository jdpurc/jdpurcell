\chapter{Conclusion}

The study found that avalanche size and area are well-modelled by the distribution family 
\[p(x) \propto x^{-\tau} e^-\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)^\beta\]

Avalanche area appeared to obey finite scaling with $c = k L^D$, and we obtained estimates of the scaling coefficient $\tau_a \approx 1.02$ and fractal dimension $D_a \approx 1.97$. The latter is consistent with previous work \cite{tebaldi1999multifractal}, which suggests $D_a \approx 2$, but the former is somewhat lower than their calculation $\tau_a = 1.2$. Further analysis is required to obtain confidence intervals for the estimated parameters, to test whether $\tau_a=1.2$ is plausible.

While we obtained sensible estimates $D_s \approx 2.2$ and $D_d \approx 1$, reflecting the 2D and 1D nature of the statistics, the finite size scaling fit was visually less convincing than for avalanche area. For avalanche size and length, the stretching coefficient $\beta$ varied substantially depending on the lattice size $L$. While the power law coefficient remained relatively constant for avalanche size, $\tau_s \approx 1.02$, it was highly $L$-dependent for $L_1$ distance and exhibited an increasing trend.

\section{Further Study}
Further study is needed to quantify the fits obtained. Additional computation can re-run the simulations and optimisation procedure performed to obtain empirical distributions for the estimated parameters for each model, which can be used to construct confidence intervals. 

Likewise, bootstrap estimation techniques can provide an indication of the variance of the observed distributions, which can help inform the dependency structure of the data required to perform formal goodness-of-fit hypothesis tests.

Additional proposed distributions are required for avalanche length, as the distributions examined above appear inadequate.

\section{Reflection}
This project extended my knowledge in several ways. First, on the implementation side, I was unfamiliar with the \texttt{numba} library and had to think about how to convert my Python simulation into code that could be pre-compiled. In particular, this meant re-thinking how I implemented queues, counted avalanche sizes and areas, and required me to bring in data structure knowledge from my C programming experience into Python. I also had limited experience with \texttt{numpy} vectorisation, which I used throughout my implemented pmf and likelihood functions to calculate probabilities for an array of inputs. I also learned a lot about plot functions, from how to use axes to draw multiple plot figures, to visualising asymptotes.

Second, this project made me think carefully about how we can statistically justify certain estimation procedures, especially regression of log-log plots. While this appears correct, once I carefully considered the assumptions of the linear model, it was clearly not justifiable. I learned about various power law distributions, derived the log likelihood functions, and thought about how to adjust the distributional assumption to capture variation that was not well-explained by the previous model, such as introducing the exponential truncation and stretching coefficients. I also had to justify the estimation technique from dependent samples using the Markov Chain formulation. 

Finally, I increased my understanding of the abelian sandpiles model and self-organised criticality. I drew connections between my statistical models and finite size scaling, and thought about how I could assess finite size scaling by imposing restrictions on my model parameters. When the results showed that avalanche size and area differ in their pooled parameter estimation, I proposed the interpretation that avalanche size should decay more slowly as $L$ increases, as opportunities for repeat topples increase.
